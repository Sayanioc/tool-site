What this converter does

It converts a pressure between the units found on plant P&IDs, transmitter nameplates and gauge dials, and shows the same pressure as gauge (relative to local atmosphere) and absolute (relative to perfect vacuum).

Gauge vs absolute pressure

P(abs) = P(gauge) + P(atm)
P(atm) ≈ 101.325 kPa at sea level; ≈ 89.9 kPa at 1000 m altitude

Mark every pressure as barg/bara or psig/psia. Absolute pressure is needed for vapour pressure, NPSH, gas density and flow compensation; gauge pressure is what most field gauges and transmitters show. Differential pressure (DP) has no reference – convert it like a gauge value.

Conversion factors

1 bar      = 100 000 Pa            (exact)
1 psi      = 6 894.757 293 Pa      (1 lbf / in², exact)
1 kg/cm²   = 98 066.5 Pa           (kgf/cm², exact)
1 mmH₂O    = 9.806 65 Pa           (conventional, ρ = 1000 kg/m³)
1 inH₂O    = 249.088 91 Pa         (conventional, ρ = 1000 kg/m³)
1 mmHg     = 133.322 387 Pa        (conventional, ρ = 13 595.1 kg/m³)
1 atm      = 101 325 Pa            (exact)
1 Torr     = 101 325 / 760 Pa      (≈ mmHg, differs in the 7th digit)

Worked example

A pump discharge gauge reads 12 kg/cm². In bar: 12 × 0.980665 = 11.768 barg. In psi: 12 × 14.2233 = 170.68 psig. Absolute at sea level: 11.768 + 1.01325 = 12.781 bara.

Common mistakes

  • Treating kg/cm² as equal to bar – it is 1.9 % smaller (1 kg/cm² = 0.980665 bar).
  • Mixing gauge and absolute: a 0–10 bara transmitter reads ~1 bar at atmosphere, not 0.
  • inH₂O reference temperature: transmitter makers may use water at 68 °F (1 inH₂O = 248.64 Pa) or 60 °F (248.84 Pa) instead of the conventional 249.089 Pa used here – about 0.2 % difference. Check the transmitter's configured unit.
  • Using standard atmosphere at high-altitude sites when converting to absolute.
